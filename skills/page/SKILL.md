# Page

A page is the map, seen. Someone looks at it, with no one talking, and gets the whole without effort: the page carries the thinking so they do not have to. The same page works for the owner's own notes and for a report shown to others, for a reader who knows the field and one who does not. The form is whatever they will look at it in, and the form changes only the layout. What never changes is the map's names, its cut, its order, and its plain words.

## Structure

The page is the map in order. A title, then one line that says the point. Then the frame, drawn: the few things the whole is made of, seen before anything is read. Then one section per part of the frame, numbered, in the map's order, so each section follows from the one before it the way a proof does.

A section is a title that says its point, one line that opens it, and the visual that carries it. It makes its own point and no other. What belongs to a later section waits for it.

The surface holds what the reader needs to act or to voice over, and nothing else. What they would open, a maker, a stage, a workflow, shows its name and its one line and opens on a click to what is inside: the idea, the thing in and the thing out, the proof, the steps. The rest stays in the map. A surface that holds depth is not finished, and neither is one that holds everything the map holds.

A page already approved is revised, not rebuilt. New facts go into that approved page once, each in the one place it belongs; a fact said again where it already lives is a regression, not completeness.

A page whose look matters to a room is not built in full on the first direction. Sketch two or three different looks, cheap and side by side, let the owner pick, and when more than one maker sketches, give each only the goal and one distinguishing lens, never a layout.

## Elements

Each element has one job. Use the one whose job matches, and no element for show.

- A big number with its label, for one measure.
- A strip to scale, for a whole cut into kinds.
- A tile with an image, a mark, a number, a name and one line, for one part among its siblings.
- A table with marks, yes, partly, no, for rows that share the same columns.
- One bordered line, for a distinction worth holding.
- Two boxes side by side, for a contrast.
- Stage boxes with labelled arrows, for a flow and what moves between its stages.
- In, maker, out, for what goes in and what comes out.
- Measures beside the thing they measure, cost, time, length, next to the demo.
- A toggle, for two things that share one slot.
- A bar to scale, for a comparison.
- A chip, for a name the reader meets again.
- An image strip, in to out, for a workflow in action.
- A footnote in small type, for a source or a caveat.

The real things are gathered before anything is drawn: the screen, the output, the log, the number. Where one does not exist yet, run the system to make it, or mark the slot and leave it empty. An open question is marked where it sits.

## Look

The ground is quiet so the content is what the eye sees. One accent. A few colours that each mean one thing and mean it everywhere, so a kind or a stage is known by its colour before it is read. Three type roles: a bold display for titles, a readable body, a small plain type for labels, numbers and notes. Level is seen in size and kind in shape, so the whole, a part and a note are told apart before they are read; a page where everything looks the same has no distinction. Nothing is there to decorate. It looks like something a person designed with care, and it is nice to look at because nothing on it is in the way.

## Balance

Prose carries the flow. One line opens a section, one line sits under a name, and that is where the sentences are. Visuals carry the facts. A paragraph where a line would do is too much; a page of only visuals has no flow and is too little. Words in boxes are still words, a chip is for a name and not a way to list, and a label or mark that already says something is not said again beside it.

## Judged by looking

A maker cannot see its own page. Render it and look at it as the viewer. Then two second eyes. One holds the map and checks that nothing the map holds is missing and that each section follows from the last. One sees only the render, knowing nothing, and says what the page is about, where it had to think, and what is on it that the picture did not need. Fix, look again, until the picture alone tells the whole and the owner would put it up in place of one they made themselves.

A third eye reads only the claims: every number, caption and label has to hold against the real process behind it and agree with everything else the page says — a real frame that could pass for AI output fails the same way a number that quietly leaves something out does.

A real screen of a real system gets a fourth eye, at the zoom a viewer would actually use: nothing a blur or a cover was meant to hide survives it, and nothing standing beside the hidden name still points at who it is — a system, a headcount, a named peer, anything that identifies by proxy.
