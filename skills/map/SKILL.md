---
name: map
description: Use whenever an understanding has to be held or handed over, a system, a project, a change, a plan, a pitch, a schema, a result, anything with more pieces than fit in one head, so that the reader leaves holding the whole and able to explain it to someone else. Run it before any page, deck, doc, diagram or ticket set, since those are drawn from the map. Trigger on "map", "help me understand", "explain this", "organize my thoughts", "I have a lot of ideas", or a messy brain dump.
---

<the-point>

A map is a simple, straightforward frame that anyone can carry: the whole, the few parts that make it, and each part in its place. The parts are MECE, mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive: none overlaps another, and together they cover everything. It is built from the owner's thoughts, ideas, facts and unknowns, the whole orchestra of their context, and worked through with them until you both hold the same shape. It is written top down, like a mathematical proof: plain and in order, each line giving the reader what they need for the next. Done well, someone who was not there and has never worked in the domain follows it with ease and can explain it to someone else, and the owner, back after weeks away, picks up as if they never left.

</the-point>

<the-frame>

The frame is the whole and its few parts, and it comes first. It comes from the context: what the owner says, what they know for sure, their questions, their gaps, the code, the files, etc. What already exists is never the frame, only more context. The whole is the big idea the owner is after, not the individual turn in front of you.

The owner arrives with many thoughts at once. Do not answer them one by one. Find the one whole they all belong to and say it back the way you would to a friend, top down like a proof, in their words: name the parts in a breath, then explain each one in that order. Keep it simple and straightforward. If more than one frame fits, show each and let them choose. Then work it with them, as many turns as it takes.

When something new comes in, do not add a part for it. Look for the higher level that holds it. MECE means fewer, better parts, not more. The frame is right when it can be drawn in one picture and every item has exactly one home.

</the-frame>

<the-parts>

The sections of the map are the parts of the frame, nothing else. Anything that runs across all of them, where it runs, what it costs, goes inside each part, never as a section of its own.

To check the parts, imagine handing the map as it stands to someone who was not there. Could they make sense of it? If there is still a gap, a part is missing. If there are too many parts, or parts that do not connect to each other, the frame is wrong, not the parts: go up a level and make it simpler. Each part says, in one line, why it exists.

</the-parts>

<each-thing-in-its-place>

The idea in one line. Then one line per part. Then each part opened the same way. Say each thing once, where it belongs, and nowhere earlier. Keep only what the reader needs to hold the whole. Detail stays where it lives, in the code, the data, the files; the map points to it. Use one name for one thing, everywhere. The whole map fits in one glance.

</each-thing-in-its-place>

<keeping-it>

After the map is made, new facts and corrections come in. Each one should fit into the frame. If it fits, change the line it belongs to, and the pieces under it follow. If it does not fit, the frame is wrong, so fix the frame. Prefer sharpening a line that is already there. Add a new part only when nothing can be sharpened to cover it. Never append.

</keeping-it>

<done>

The owner is rest assured: every thought of theirs is in it, and the high-level logic holds, so everything built under it, map within map, will work out without them going into the fine details. Someone who was not there, even in a domain new to them or more technical than they know, reads it and is on the exact same train of thought, with nothing to work out and nowhere to deviate.

</done>
